---
title: "Not all extremal directions are the same kind of thing"
subtitle: "Sampling Conway and Shipman's proof space with a language model"
date: 2026-08-15
---

Conway and Shipman propose treating proofs of a theorem as points in a space
whose coordinates are attributes — brevity, elementarity, generality,
visuality, weight of machinery — and identifying *extreme proofs* as the
vertices of that space. They built theirs by hand, from proofs collected out
of the literature.

I asked whether a language model can be used to sample it. Generate proofs
under prompts that each maximise one attribute, embed them, and see where
they land relative to a labelled reference set of known proofs.

The short version: the attributes do not behave like coordinates of one
space. Three of them select among known arguments. One leaves the known set
entirely. One changes nothing about the mathematics and only redraws it.

I found the last two by reading the proofs. Two separate metrics told me
something else, and both were wrong.

---

## The corpus

450 proofs of the infinitude of primes, generated by `claude-opus-5` (medium
effort, pinned snapshot, one model throughout) via the Message Batches API,
in two arms.

**Technique arm, 300 records.** Five known proofs (Euclid, Euler product,
Fürstenberg, Erdős counting, Fermat numbers) × six languages (en, fr, de,
es, zh, ja) × two styles (terse, verbose) × five samples. Technique is
prescribed in the prompt, so these carry ground-truth labels. This arm is
the *instrument*: labelled interior points against which any measurement can
be validated.

**Extreme arm, 150 records.** Five extremal criteria × six languages × five
samples. Technique is not prescribed — the model chooses.

| criterion | prompt asks for |
|---|---|
| brevity | the shortest proof, subject to correctness |
| elementarity | assume nothing beyond divisibility; quote no named theorem |
| generality | the argument whose method extends furthest |
| visuality | describable as a picture or construction |
| machinery | quote the heaviest available named theorems |

`elementarity` and `machinery` are deliberately opposed, so the sampled
region has width along at least one axis rather than collapsing to a point.
Style appears only in the technique arm — adding terse/verbose to the
extreme arm would confound directly with `brevity`.

Chinese and Japanese are in the grid deliberately. A European-only design
would let shared Latin roots (prime/premier/primo) give a multilingual
embedding an easy ride; two non-Indo-European scripts with no cognate
vocabulary make cross-lingual claims much harder to fake. That turned out to
matter — see the transfer numbers below.

Language is otherwise an intended nuisance factor. The design assumes
averaging over six languages removes variation in phrasing while leaving
variation in argument. Testing that assumption is most of Part 1.

### One corpus intervention, disclosed

The first generation run capped output at 10,000 tokens and produced 28
truncations, concentrated overwhelmingly in `generality` (83% of the
truncations) and `machinery` (10%). The cause was a clause in the
`generality` prompt asking the model to "make that extension visible" — a
request for a deliverable *beyond* the proof, which inflated the output past
the cap.

I identified this from the prompt text before reading any of the outputs,
removed the clause, raised the cap, and regenerated the affected cells: 33
records, 30 `generality` and 3 `machinery`. I take this to be a
specification error fixed like a typo, not tuning on results — the diagnosis
came from the prompt, not from what the proofs said. But it affects the two
directions that carry the main findings below, so it belongs here rather
than in a footnote. Judge it accordingly.

The intervention is checkable from the corpus file without taking my word
for it: records from the first run carry no `max_tokens` field, and the
regenerated ones carry `max_tokens: 32000`. Every record stores its full
prompt, model, effort, token cap and usage. The grid is balanced, which is
what licenses the mean-subtractions below.

# Part 1: building the instrument

Two embedding models appear below. An earlier pilot used
`paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2` (768-dim) on the technique arm
alone; the current analysis uses `BAAI/bge-m3` (1024-dim, 8192-token
context) on all 450 records. I flag which is which throughout, because on
two measurements they disagree and both disagreements are informative.

The context length matters: a short-context multilingual model truncates
every proof to its opening paragraph, and since length correlates with
criterion, that truncation would be systematic rather than noise.

## Manipulation check: does the apparatus respond at all?

Character counts are not comparable across scripts — within every direction
the CJK-to-Latin median ratio is ≈0.57, and the stability of that ratio
across all five directions identifies it as a script property rather than a
proof property. The effect is multiplicative, so correction happens in log
space: $z_i = (\log L_i - \mu_\ell)/\sigma_\ell$, with $\mu_\ell,
\sigma_\ell$ over all 75 records in language $\ell$.

| criterion | mean $z$ | spread across 6 languages |
|---|---|---|
| brevity | −1.80 | 0.40 |
| elementarity | −0.04 | 0.28 |
| generality | +0.28 | 0.36 |
| visuality | +0.33 | 0.25 |
| machinery | +0.86 | 0.32 |

Between-criterion range 2.66 against a mean within-criterion spread of 0.32,
roughly 8:1.

This is a check, not a finding. Asked for the shortest proof, the model
produced the shortest proof. It confirms the criteria reach the generator
and that length is measurable as a coordinate once normalised. It discovers
nothing. Note that three criteria fall within 0.37 $z$ of each other and are
unseparated by this axis — which is why a second coordinate is needed before
any of this is geometry.

One interaction survives the normalisation: **CJK × brevity**, a −0.32 $z$
gap (Latin −1.69, CJK −2.02). The shortest proof in Chinese or Japanese is
shorter, relative to that language's own baseline, than the shortest proof
in a European language. Plausibly a compression floor — the terse Euclid
argument is close to irreducible, and logographic scripts start nearer that
floor — but I have not tested it.

## Language is additive and removable

Embedding all 450 with `bge-m3`; scoring on the 300 labelled records. Mean
cosine similarity, raw and after subtracting each language's mean:

| | raw | centred |
|---|---|---|
| same technique, different language | +0.735 | +0.275 |
| different technique, same language | +0.723 | −0.103 |
| **gap** | **+0.012** | **+0.378** |

In the raw space this is a tie: whether two proofs share an argument tells
you essentially nothing next to whether they share a language. After
centring, a proof is far closer to the same argument in a foreign language
than to a different argument in its own. A linear probe for language drops
from 1.000 to 0.013 — below chance — while technique classification stays at
1.000, including leave-one-language-out.

**This is not a new result.** It is Libovický, Rosa and Fraser,
[*On the Language Neutrality of Pre-trained Multilingual
Representations*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05160) (Findings of EMNLP
2020), who hypothesise that a sentence representation is additively composed
of a language-specific component and a language-neutral one, and remove the
former by subtracting a per-language centroid. I reproduced it without
knowing it existed. More on that below.

Worth adding: plain centring forces each language mean to exactly zero, so
the probe collapse is guaranteed by construction rather than discovered.
Re-running with leave-one-out means — each record centred by the mean of the
other 49 records in its language — gives identical numbers (gap +0.361,
language probe 0.013). Not an artefact of in-sample centring.

## Cross-lingual transfer: the encoder, not the centring

Train the technique probe on English records only, then apply it frozen to
each other language. This is the strongest single test of whether the
technique direction encodes mathematics or vocabulary: English and Japanese
share no words and no script, so a direction that separates Euclid from
Fürstenberg in both cannot be riding on English terms.

On the earlier pilot (`paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2`, uncentred,
chance 0.200) the European-to-CJK degradation was large. Repeating the same
frozen-probe test on `bge-m3`, also uncentred, it is largely gone:

| target | mpnet (768-dim) | bge-m3 (1024-dim) |
|---|---|---|
| French | ≈0.80–0.84 | 0.980 |
| German | ≈0.80–0.84 | 1.000 |
| Spanish | ≈0.80–0.84 | 1.000 |
| Chinese | 0.62 | 0.900 |
| Japanese | 0.50 | 0.920 |

Both columns are uncentred, so this isolates the encoder. On mpnet roughly a
third of the technique signal was riding on shared Indo-European
vocabulary — visible only because zh and ja were in the design. On `bge-m3`
that penalty shrinks to 0.08–0.10, *before* any centring is applied. So when
language-centred leave-one-language-out accuracy on `bge-m3` comes out at
1.000 including Japanese, the centring is not what closes the gap. A better
cross-lingual aligner is.

This matters for how much the transfer result is worth. It is a fact about
`bge-m3`'s multilingual alignment, and it does not by itself establish that
the aligned thing is argument structure rather than terminology — which is
the next section. Note also that this is the *harder* version of the test:
training on English alone rather than on five languages at once. Leave-one-
language-out uncentred can only do better.

## What the technique signal actually is

Two stories are consistent with everything above. Either the embedding
encodes argument structure, or it encodes topic vocabulary — "topology" for
Fürstenberg, "harmonic" for Euler, "squarefree" for Erdős — which a
multilingual model aligns across languages by design.

A lexical baseline discriminates. Within English only, classify technique
from TF-IDF features with no semantics at all, training on terse records and
testing on verbose (and vice versa), so no prompt cell appears on both
sides:

| representation | accuracy |
|---|---|
| TF-IDF, word 1–2 gram | 0.940 |
| TF-IDF, char 3–5 gram | 0.820 |
| `bge-m3` embedding | 0.980 |

(chance 0.200)

A word counter gets 94%. Within a language, technique identity is carried
almost entirely by terminology. That word n-grams beat character n-grams
suggests the signal is whole technical terms rather than orthographic
pattern.

This is the finding that constrains everything downstream. The transfer
result above says `bge-m3` aligns the technique signal across scripts; this
says the signal being aligned is largely lexical. For Part 1 that is a
limitation on what the cross-language result means. For Part 2 it turns out
to be the thing that matters most, twice.

## A result that did not survive its null

Principal angles between the factor subspaces come out near-orthogonal:
technique versus language at min 78.9°, technique versus style at min 82.4°.
The obvious reading is that the three design factors share almost no
directions — stronger than mere separability.

That reading is wrong.

| | tech vs language | tech vs style |
|---|---|---|
| observed min angle | 78.9° | 82.4° |
| permuted-label null | 77.0° | 80.9° |
| random-subspace null | 83.0° | 85.2° |
| p(null ≤ observed), permuted | 0.86 | 0.69 |

The permuted null shuffles technique labels *within each (language, style)
cell*, preserving the balance of the grid. It lands at 77.0° against an
observed 78.9°: shuffling the labels barely moves the angle, so the angle is
not a fact about technique. The random-subspace null sits at 83.0°, which is
simply what two subspaces of this rank do in 1024 dimensions — and the
observed angles are slightly *below* it, meaning the factors share
marginally more structure than chance. The opposite of the claim.

Both nulls take about ten seconds on cached embeddings. I would not have
found this by staring at the number.

## Four metrics, four questions

The earlier pilot produced numbers that looked contradictory until I sorted
them by what they actually ask:

| metric | question | answer for technique |
|---|---|---|
| linear probe accuracy | is the information present? | 0.923, emphatically yes |
| k-means ARI / NMI | does it dominate the geometry? | ARI 0.235 — no; style does |
| silhouette | are the groups cleanly separated? | 0.061 — nothing is |
| cross-lingual transfer | is it language-independent? | mostly for European, half lost for ja |

No contradiction. The composite statement: technique information is robustly
encoded along recoverable linear directions, is not the principal axis of
variation, and is partially entangled with vocabulary.

The dominant axis in the raw space is *style register* — terse versus
verbose. That is an artefact of my prompt design, not a property of proof
space. I put it there.

## A prediction made in advance, half-confirmed

Before running the pilot I predicted a dissociation: mathematical notation
($p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n + 1$, $\sum 1/n$) is shared across all six languages,
and the terse style is notation-heavy while verbose is prose-heavy. So terse
proofs should cluster by technique more strongly, *and* verbose proofs
should carry more language signal — the two moving in opposite directions.

On the pilot encoder both halves appeared to hold (technique NMI: terse
0.684, verbose 0.543; language probe: terse 0.673, verbose 0.873). On
`bge-m3` only the first half survives:

| slice | technique NMI | language probe |
|---|---|---|
| terse | 0.754 | 0.993 |
| verbose | 0.567 | 1.000 |

The technique half replicates and strengthens: the terse-minus-verbose NMI
gap grows from +0.141 to +0.187. Notation does carry the mathematics.

The language half does not replicate at all. On `bge-m3` the language probe
saturates in both slices — 0.993 against 1.000 is not a dissociation, it is
a ceiling. Language is perfectly recoverable from a terse proof too. So
"prose carries the language" was a statement about a weaker encoder's
limitations, not about prose. I am keeping the prediction in because I made
it in advance and half of it was wrong in a way I would not have noticed
without re-running it on a second model.

**Supporting detail.** Language is low-dimensional but not
one-dimensional: SVD of the six language means gives variance fractions
0.556 / 0.160 / 0.106 / 0.096 / 0.082, with the leading direction ordering
zh(−0.29), ja(−0.27), de(+0.10), en(+0.15), es(+0.15), fr(+0.16) — a
CJK-versus-Latin script split with the European languages nearly
coincident. A 2D UMAP shows that split cleanly and *looks*
one-dimensional; it is not, the projection just shows the largest
component. Variance shares of the total are technique 0.240, language
0.222, style 0.090 — argument does not dominate the raw space — and the
joint technique × style cells account for 0.396 against 0.330 if the two
were additive, so style is not a uniform offset applied to every technique.

**Technique with language held fixed.** Restricting to one language at a
time, technique still clusters, but by very different amounts:

| language | NMI | ARI | silhouette |
|---|---|---|---|
| en | 0.719 | 0.537 | 0.235 |
| ja | 0.721 | 0.543 | 0.194 |
| fr | 0.595 | 0.337 | 0.222 |
| de | 0.521 | 0.248 | 0.227 |
| es | 0.479 | 0.187 | 0.217 |
| zh | 0.449 | 0.135 | 0.178 |

Japanese and English are the two cleanest, and Chinese the least clean by a
wide margin — which is not what a story about "CJK is harder for the
encoder" predicts, since ja and zh land at opposite ends. I have no
explanation for it. (The linear probe reads 1.000 in every language and is
omitted: 50 records in 1024 dimensions are separable more or less
regardless of the labels, so that column carries no information.)

# Part 2: sampling the vertices

Classify all 150 extreme-arm records against the five language-centred
technique centroids.

|  | erdős | euclid | euler | fermat | fürstenberg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| brevity | 0 | **30** | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| elementarity | 0 | **30** | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| generality | 0 | 0 | **30** | 0 | 0 |
| machinery | 0 | 0 | 4 | 0 | **26** |
| visuality | **23** | 2 | 0 | 1 | 4 |

Direction and technique choice are nowhere near independent. The textbook
test gives $\chi^2 = 365.5$, dof 16, Cramér's V = 0.781, but with 25 cells
and a `fermat` column containing one record in total, several expected
counts are far below where the asymptotic $p$-value means anything. So the
same statistic against a permutation null instead — shuffling the direction
labels against the assigned techniques, 20,000 times, which assumes nothing
about cell counts:

| | value |
|---|---|
| observed $\chi^2$ | 365.5 |
| permutation null, mean | 16.1 |
| permutation null, max over 20,000 | 44.9 |
| $p$ | $< 1/20{,}000$ |

Not one shuffle in twenty thousand comes within a factor of eight of the
observed statistic. The effect was never in doubt; it now rests on a test
whose assumptions this table actually satisfies.

The mapping is also stable across languages, though not uniformly so. For
brevity, elementarity and generality it is *identical* in all six — Euclid
5/5, Euclid 5/5, Euler 5/5, in every language. For the other two it varies
at the edges: machinery picks Fürstenberg 3–5 times out of 5 depending on
language (de 3, en 4, fr 4, es/ja/zh 5), and visuality picks Erdős in five
languages but Fürstenberg in English. Whatever residual language signal
survives centring, it is not driving the three unanimous rows.

At which point the result looks clean, and it is mostly wrong.

## An out-of-set check, and how it misled me

Nearest-centroid must return one of five. A record whose proof is none of
the five still gets assigned. So: calibrate against the technique arm's own
leave-one-out self-similarity, and flag extreme-arm records that fall below
its 5th percentile.

Technique arm, cosine to own centroid (LOO): mean +0.522, 5th percentile
+0.336. Extreme arm, cosine to nearest centroid: mean +0.278.

| direction | below threshold | mean cos |
|---|---|---|
| brevity | 0/30 | +0.456 |
| elementarity | 22/30 | +0.283 |
| generality | 21/30 | +0.304 |
| visuality | 26/30 | +0.191 |
| machinery | 29/30 | +0.158 |

Brevity matters here as a positive control. The obvious objection is that
extreme-arm records differ from technique-arm records in prompt structure,
so a lower cosine could be an arm effect rather than genuine out-of-set.
Brevity refutes that: an extreme-arm record *can* sit comfortably inside the
reference distribution.

I read this as "four of five directions leave the reference set." Then I
read the proofs, and that was wrong too.

## Reading the proofs

Twelve records, three per direction, English.

**Machinery — genuinely outside the set.** The proofs invoke the Chebotarev
density theorem via class field theory, the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem
(to show $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$ is irrational, contradicting a finite Euler
product), and the Prime Number Theorem via Wiener–Ikehara. None of these is
one of the five techniques.

The classifier said Fürstenberg, 26/30, because **Fürstenberg is the most
abstract-vocabulary member of the reference set** — ideals, topologies,
clopen sets. But Fürstenberg's proof is not heavy machinery at all. It is
Euclid's argument in topological dress, using no theorem of topology, only
definitions. The model gave the mathematically correct answer to "quote the
heaviest machinery"; my instrument could not tell "uses abstract
terminology" from "uses powerful theorems."

The geometry confirms this, on both encoders. Ranking the five techniques by
mean centroid distance from all the others, **Fürstenberg comes out most
extreme** — 0.473 on the pilot, 0.500 on `bge-m3`, with Euclid most central
at 0.399. The geometry marks Fürstenberg extreme because its vocabulary is
unusual, not because its mathematics is unusual. The same property that made
it look extreme is the property that made it a magnet for genuinely heavy
proofs. That the ranking survives an encoder change makes it a fact about
the corpus rather than about one embedding.

That is the Part 1 lexical result producing a concrete misclassification.

**Generality — correctly Euler product, but the interesting part is how.**
All three build the Euler product from scratch and each explicitly notes
that *uniqueness* of prime factorisation is not needed — only existence.
Each closes with a quantitative complement showing $\sum_p 1/p$ diverges.
That is exactly what "the argument whose method extends furthest" should
produce: the same proof, stripped to its weakest hypotheses.

**Elementarity — Euclid, at length.** $P = p_1\cdots p_k$, $N = P+1$, a
common divisor divides 1. Identical argument to the brevity records, but
with every divisibility fact proved from the definition. Caveat: the prompt
*instructs* this ("assume nothing beyond divisibility, quote no named
theorem"), so the elaboration is partly prompt-induced rather than an
intrinsic property of the attribute.

**Visuality — the interesting one.** Of three records, two are Euclid:

- One lays a *comb* along the number line for each prime, teeth spaced $p$
  apart. If the primes were finite, the stack of combs would be periodic
  with period $P = \prod p_i$, so the untouched dot at 1 would repeat at
  $1+P$ — but every dot ≥ 2 is touched.
- One represents integers as *rods* of unit blocks, with $d$ tiling $n$ iff
  $d \mid n$, builds $L = \prod p_i$ by repeated magnification, glues on one
  extra block, and observes no $p_i$-rod can end at $L+1$.

Both are $P+1$ with a picture attached. The mathematics is unchanged.

Only the third is a genuine second argument: Erdős, drawn as a pegboard —
each $n \le N$ written $a^2 b$ with $b$ squarefree, pinned to a board of
$2^k$ columns and $\sqrt N$ rows, which is too small once $N > 4^k$.

So the low cosines on elementarity, generality and visuality do not indicate
out-of-set proofs. They indicate **out-of-register** ones: the same argument
in different clothes. A lexical instrument cannot separate those.

## The finding

Corrected table:

| direction | argument | kind of axis |
|---|---|---|
| brevity | Euclid | selects among known arguments |
| elementarity | Euclid, elaborated | selects among known arguments |
| generality | Euler product, weakened hypotheses | selects among known arguments |
| machinery | Chebotarev / L–W / PNT | leaves the reference set |
| visuality | Euclid as combs, Euclid as rods, one Erdős | re-renders an argument |

Conway and Shipman treat these as coordinates of one attribute space. They
are not the same kind of thing:

1. **Brevity, elementarity and generality are argument-selecting.** They
   pick among proofs that already exist, consistently, in every language.
2. **Machinery is unbounded and set-escaping.** There is always something
   heavier to quote. It is not a coordinate on a bounded region; it is a
   direction you can walk along forever.
3. **Visuality is a presentation axis, not an argument axis.** Asked for the
   most visual proof, the model mostly does not find a new argument. It
   redraws Euclid.

And brevity and elementarity are the *same vertex* at different lengths —
same argument, unanimously, in all six languages, differing only in how much
is spelled out.

A convex hull over these five axes would be a hull over three different
kinds of object. That seems to me a real objection to the framing, and it is
not visible from the geometry — only from the text.

## What I got wrong

Four claims formed and abandoned, in order:

1. **"The factors occupy near-orthogonal subspaces."** Killed by a
   permuted-label null. It was measuring dimensionality.
2. **"The cross-language result shows argument structure survives
   translation."** Bounded by a lexical baseline. Most likely it shows
   multilingual term alignment.
3. **"Four of five directions leave the reference set."** Killed by reading
   twelve proofs. Only one does.
4. **"Terse carries the mathematics, verbose carries the language."** Half
   killed by changing encoder. The technique half replicated and grew; the
   language half was an artefact of the pilot encoder's weaker language
   probe, which saturates on `bge-m3`.

And a fifth, of a different kind: the centring result is a 2020 paper. I
searched the literature along the *framing* axis — proof geometry, proof
diversity, embeddings of proofs — and found nothing close, which was
correct. I did not search along the *method* axis — multilingual embeddings,
language subspace, centroid subtraction — where the answer was six years
old. The method determines whether a result is new. Search it before writing
the code, not after.

## Limitations

- **The clouds interpenetrate.** Within-technique spread is 0.476–0.511
  while between-centroid distances are 0.399–0.500 — the spread is as large
  as the separation, and larger for every technique except Fürstenberg. The
  pilot showed the same thing (0.47–0.55 against 0.36–0.47). "Most extreme
  technique" is a ranking of centroid distances, not a vertex of a hull, and
  nothing here has fitted a hull. I have avoided the word deliberately.
- **One theorem.** One theorem gives one space. The interesting claim would
  be that brevity and machinery oppose each other the same way for $\sqrt 2$
  as for the primes, and that is untested.
- **Two embedding models.** They disagree on cross-lingual transfer and on
  the style dissociation. The transfer disagreement I have now isolated to
  the encoder; the additivity result in particular could still be a `bge-m3`
  property, since I have only tested it on one model.
- **Correctness unverified.** Nobody has checked that all 450 proofs are
  correct. I read twelve. The machinery proofs quote theorems whose own
  proofs I have not checked for circularity — the records themselves argue
  they are not circular, and I have not verified that argument.
- **The generator is the subject.** Everything here is a fact about one
  model's organisation of proof-writing, not about proofs. That is a
  different and narrower question than the one Conway and Shipman asked.
- **Fermat numbers is selected once in 150 records.** An interior point that
  no extremal direction reaches. I have no explanation.
- **Language-held-fixed clustering varies more than I can explain.**
  Technique NMI within a single language runs from 0.449 (zh) to 0.721 (ja).
  Both are CJK, so this is not a script effect.
- **Register versus argument is not measured, only observed.** I claim
  visuality is a presentation axis on the basis of three English records. A
  proper test would need a measure that separates the two — which is exactly
  what this corpus shows the embedding does not provide.

One limitation from an earlier draft has been resolved and is worth
recording as such: I had flagged `euler_product` as the most-confused
technique on the pilot encoder (10 of 23 probe errors, Euclid the most
common false prediction), unsure whether that was corpus quality or genuine
centrality of Euclid. On `bge-m3` the technique probe misclassifies 0 of
300. It was the encoder.

## What would come next

Two things, in order of cost.

**Cheap.** A second coordinate that is not lexical: **the count of
externally-invoked results** — lemmas and prior theorems cited rather than
proved. Fürstenberg invokes topology axioms; Euclid invokes almost nothing;
the Chebotarev proof invokes a great deal. Countable from an abstracted step
sequence, script-independent, and not a monotone function of length. With
that plus normalised length you would have two genuine coordinates, and
hull-fitting would start to mean something.

**Hard.** "Same argument, different presentation" has no measurement in this
pipeline at all. The visuality records make that concrete: combs, rods, and
$P+1$ are one point in proof space and three points in embedding space.
Until that is fixed, sampled geometry measures writing as much as it
measures mathematics.
