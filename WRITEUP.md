---
title: "Not all extremal directions are the same kind of thing"
subtitle: "Sampling Conway and Shipman's proof space with a language model, on three theorems"
date: 2026-08-23
---

Conway and Shipman propose treating proofs of a theorem as points in a space
whose coordinates are attributes — brevity, elementarity, generality,
visuality, weight of machinery — and identifying *extreme proofs* as the
vertices of that space. They built theirs by hand, from proofs collected out
of the literature.

I asked whether a language model can be used to sample it. Generate proofs
under prompts that each maximise one attribute, embed them, and see where
they land relative to a labelled reference set of known proofs.

The short version, now on three theorems rather than one: the attributes do
not behave like coordinates of one space, and *which* attribute misbehaves
depends on the theorem. The apparatus is real — language is removable, the
direction prompt genuinely changes which argument the model reaches for, and
both replicate cleanly. Almost everything built on top of the *embedding*
turned out to be measuring vocabulary.

---

## What changed since the first version

The first version of this ran on one theorem, 450 proofs of the infinitude
of primes, and reported five results. Three of them survive. This version
adds two more theorems and three more extremal directions, and the second
and third theorems are what make the difference: a result that appears on
one theorem and not the others is a fact about that theorem, and there was
no way to see that before.

The headline change is that **the technique signal is lexical**, and the
second and third theorems make that much harder to argue with. On √2, a bag
of words *beats* the encoder outright.

---

## The corpus

1,944 proofs across three theorems, generated by `claude-opus-5` (medium
effort, pinned snapshot, one model throughout) via the Message Batches API.

| theorem | total | technique | extreme | scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| infinitude of primes | 648 | 300 (5 × 6 × 2 × 5) | 240 (8 × 6 × 5) | 108 |
| irrationality of √2 | 726 | 360 (6 × 6 × 2 × 5) | 240 | 126 |
| Pythagorean theorem | 708 | 360 (6 × 6 × 2 × 5) | 240 | 108 |

Three arms per theorem:

**Technique arm.** Known proofs × six languages (en, fr, de, es, zh, ja) ×
two styles (terse, verbose) × five samples. Technique is prescribed, so
these carry ground-truth labels. This arm is the *instrument*: labelled
interior points against which any measurement can be validated.

**Extreme arm.** Eight extremal criteria × six languages × five samples.
Technique is not prescribed — the model chooses. This is what gets measured.

**Scope arm.** Proofs of *neighbouring statements*, with no selection
criterion. Two jobs: Conway and Shipman's scope test (as a statement moves
out of a proof's documented reach, does that proof stop being the one the
model produces?), and the missing baseline — what the model writes when
nothing is being asked of it.

Chinese and Japanese are deliberate. A European-only grid lets shared Latin
roots give a multilingual embedding an easy ride, and as it turns out the
CJK columns are where several results are decided.

Integrity is clean on all three: no duplicate ids, no truncations, no empty
proofs, one model and one effort setting throughout, no cell with a mixed
prompt.

# Part 1: building the instrument

## Manipulation check: does the apparatus respond at all?

Before any embedding, the crudest possible measurement: character count,
normalised within language (log length, z-scored per language, because
script density is multiplicative and a Chinese proof of the same content is
roughly 0.57× the length of the English one).

The direction prompts move it, and by a lot more than language does:

| theorem | between-direction range | mean within-direction spread | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| primes | 2.88 | 0.33 | **8.8 : 1** |
| √2 | 2.61 | 0.26 | **9.9 : 1** |
| Pythagoras | 2.91 | 0.37 | **7.8 : 1** |

That ratio is the assumption the six-language design rests on — that
language is a nuisance factor on this axis — and it holds on all three.
`brevity` is the extreme in every case (z ≈ −1.7 to −1.8), `machinery` at
the other end.

**The centre cell changes the reading, and differs by theorem.** The scope
arm's first target is the theorem itself with no criterion, which is the
origin every direction should be extreme *relative to*:

| theorem | unprompted z | brevity vs centre | machinery vs centre |
|---|---|---|---|
| primes | −0.40 | −1.57 | +1.31 |
| √2 | −0.45 | −1.21 | +1.41 |
| Pythagoras | −0.01 | −1.80 | +0.79 |

On primes and √2 the model writes *shorter* than the grid mean when
unprompted, so most directions are lengthening. On Pythagoras the
unprompted length sits exactly at the grid mean, and every direction except
brevity is longer than it. Same axis, different origin.

## Language is additive and removable

This is the cleanest result in the project, and it replicates on all three
theorems.

Subtracting each language's mean embedding takes the technique probe from
1.000 to 1.000 and the *language* probe from 1.000 to **0.013** (primes).
Pairwise, the same-technique/cross-language cosine gap goes from +0.093 (a
near-tie against +0.081 for language) to **+0.361**.

The obvious objection is that centring on in-sample means guarantees the
collapse by construction. It does not: under leave-one-out means, where each
record's offset is estimated without it, the numbers are *identical* to
three decimals — gap +0.361, language probe 0.013. Not an artefact of
in-sample centring.

Leave-one-language-out technique classification, trained on five languages
and tested on the sixth: **1.000** (primes), **0.992** (√2), **0.997**
(Pythagoras). The instrument works, and it works out of sample.

This is a replication of [Libovický et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05160),
which I found after the fact. I searched the literature along the *framing*
axis — proof geometry, proof diversity, embeddings of proofs — and found
nothing close, which was correct. I did not search along the *method* axis —
multilingual embeddings, language subspace, centroid subtraction — where the
answer was six years old. Search the method before writing the code.

## Cross-lingual transfer: the encoder, not the centring

Train the technique probe on English only, apply it frozen to the other five
languages, on **raw** (uncentred) embeddings. English and Japanese share no
script and no cognate vocabulary, so a direction separating Euclid from
Fürstenberg in both is not riding on English terms.

| | en→de | en→es | en→fr | en→ja | en→zh | mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| primes | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.980 | 0.920 | 0.900 | 0.960 |
| √2 | 0.933 | 1.000 | 0.950 | 0.950 | 0.983 | 0.963 |
| Pythagoras | 0.967 | 1.000 | 0.967 | 0.950 | 0.933 | 0.963 |

An earlier 768-dimension encoder managed 0.62 and 0.50 on zh and ja. The
gap was the encoder, not the centring — and the replication across three
theorems at 0.96 makes that solid.

## A result that did not survive its null

The factor subspaces look near-orthogonal: minimum principal angle 78.9°
between technique and language, 82.4° between technique and style. That
reads like a finding about the design.

It is not. Permuting the technique labels *within each (language, style)
cell* — keeping the design intact, destroying only the labels — gives a null
at 77.0°, and p(null ≤ observed) = **0.86**. A random subspace of the same
rank sits at 83.0°.

The observed angle measures the dimensionality, not the design. In 1024
dimensions almost any two low-rank subspaces are nearly orthogonal. The null
took ten seconds to write and killed the result.

## What the technique signal actually is

Here is the number that reframes the whole project. Within English only,
holding style out (train on terse, test on verbose, so no cell appears on
both sides), can TF-IDF alone recover which technique a proof is?

| theorem | tfidf word 1-2gram | tfidf char 3-5gram | bge-m3 | chance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| primes | 0.940 | 0.820 | 0.980 | 0.200 |
| √2 | **1.000** | 0.933 | 0.933 | 0.167 |
| Pythagoras | 0.917 | **1.000** | 0.983 | 0.167 |

On primes a bag of words comes within four points of a 1024-dimensional
multilingual encoder. **On √2 it beats it.** On Pythagoras character n-grams
beat it.

That is consistent with the encoder reading arguments *and* with it reading
only vocabulary, and this section cannot separate them. The masking ablation
below is what separates them, and the answer is not comfortable.

## Taking the vocabulary away

Rewrite the corpus with the give-away terms replaced by a single neutral
placeholder, then ask the same question. Two ways to choose what to remove.

**Named machinery is not the give-away.** Masking every entry in the
registry of named results, every mathematician's surname in six languages,
and every technique-diagnostic symbol moves the lexical baseline by
*nothing* — 0.940 before, 0.940 after, on primes. It matches 1.0 to 6.5
terms per proof depending on technique, and 275 of 648 records contain no
match at all. Whatever
identifies a technique, it is not the citations.

**It is the ordinary descriptive vocabulary, and it is diffuse.** Selecting
terms by classifier weight instead — chosen on terse records, tested on
verbose, so the selection never sees the test side:

| top-k masked | primes | √2 | Pythagoras |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.960 | 1.000 | 0.833 |
| 10 | 0.800 | 0.967 | 0.767 |
| 50 | 0.600 | 0.667 | 0.833 |
| 100 | 0.400 | 0.433 | 0.767 |
| 400 | **0.200** | **0.167** | 0.433 |
| chance | 0.200 | 0.167 | 0.167 |

On primes and √2 the lexical baseline dies at ~400 word types — and 86.5%
and 89.1% of the text is still on the page when it does. Pythagoras is more
redundant and still sits at 0.433 after 400, which is why its cut is set at
800.

The terms doing the work are things like `squarefree`, `contradicting`,
`distributivity`, `contrapositive` — ordinary technical prose, not proper
nouns. This is the ambiguous outcome and worth stating as such: an argument
and the words used to state it are not separable in prose, so a gentle slope
is as consistent with the encoder tracking the mathematics as with it
tracking diffuse wording.

**The hole does not leak.** Every masked term becomes the same placeholder,
so the corpus does not record which term was removed. Classifying technique
from what a reader of the masked text *can* see — placeholder count, length,
density — sits at chance (0.200 against 0.200). An earlier version of this
check used per-term counts and reported a leak of 0.90; that was the check
classifying from its own mask log rather than from the proofs.

Masking also does not shorten proofs unevenly: length retained is 0.908–0.923
across techniques on the discriminative tier, and brevity is one of the
value functions under study, so a technique-dependent shortening would have
replaced one confound with another.

> **Not yet measured.** The masking ablation on the *embedding* — whether
> bge-m3 still recovers the technique on a corpus where TF-IDF is at chance —
> requires embedding six masked corpora, which I have not run. Everything
> above is the TF-IDF side of the question. The open question it would settle:
> if bge-m3 holds up where a bag of words has died, something beyond
> vocabulary is being represented; if it falls with the words, the technique
> geometry here is a lexical artefact and the cross-lingual transfer above
> was proper-noun alignment.

## A prediction made in advance, half-confirmed

Terse proofs are mostly notation, which is shared across all six languages
and is technique-specific. Verbose proofs are mostly prose, which is
neither. So terse records should carry more technique signal and less
language signal. Predicted before the corpus was generated.

On primes: technique NMI 0.754 terse against 0.567 verbose, a difference of
+0.187 in the predicted direction. The probe saturates at 1.000 in both, so
the unsupervised score is the only one carrying information.

The style subspace is exactly one-dimensional (100% of variance in one
direction), which is what you would expect from a two-level factor and worth
noting only because the language subspace is *not*: language occupies
genuinely multiple dimensions (0.556, 0.160, 0.106, 0.096, 0.082), with the
leading direction cleanly separating CJK from Latin — zh(−0.29), ja(−0.27)
against de/en/es/fr all positive.

# Part 2: sampling the vertices

## The finding that replicates

The extreme arm carries no technique label. Classify it against the
technique centroids and ask: does the direction prompt change which known
argument the model reaches for?

It does, on all three theorems, and the effect is enormous:

| theorem | χ² | dof | Cramér's V | permutation p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| primes | 560.0 | 28 | 0.764 | < 1/20000 |
| √2 | 330.4 | 35 | 0.525 | < 1/20000 |
| Pythagoras | 457.2 | 35 | 0.617 | < 1/20000 |

The permutation null shuffles the direction labels 20,000 times and
recomputes; the observed χ² of 560.0 on primes sits against a null mean of
28.1 and a null *maximum* of 74.5. Not one shuffle came close. This is
reported instead of the asymptotic p because several cells are tiny and the
χ² approximation would not support them.

Restricting to records whose assignment margin over the second-best centroid
exceeds 0.02 (dropping 11 of 240) barely moves it: χ² 554.7, V 0.778.

**And the mapping is the same in every language.** On primes, `brevity`,
`elementarity`, `nonvisuality` and `generality` select the identical
technique in all six languages — 5/5 in each. That is what makes it a
property of the direction rather than residual language leakage.

## What each direction actually selects

This is where the three theorems diverge, and the divergence is the result.

**Primes.** Four directions collapse onto Euclid: `brevity` 30/30,
`elementarity` 30/30, `nonvisuality` 30/30, `constructiveness` 23/30. Then
`generality` → Euler product 30/30, `surprise` → Fürstenberg 28/30,
`visuality` → Erdős counting 23/30, `machinery` → Fürstenberg 22/30 with
Euler product 8/30.

**√2.** Much flatter. `elementarity` → traditional 26/30, `surprise` →
analytic 25/30, and then things spread: `brevity` splits traditional 11 /
covering 10 / reciprocation 4, `machinery` splits analytic 11 / folding 9 /
unique factorisation 7. The article calls covering and folding "mechanically
the same", and the instrument agrees — the technique probe makes 4 errors in
360 here against 0 in 300 on primes.

**Visuality was predicted to split on √2, and it does.** This was the axis
that died on primes (three visual proofs the encoder could not tell apart).
√2 has two geometric arguments the article itself separates — covering is
"visually obvious", folding "purely geometrical" — and the direction splits
almost evenly between them: **covering 16/30, folding 14/30**. That is a
question with an answer, and the answer is that the prompt does not choose
between them.

**Pythagoras.** The sharpest mapping of the three. `machinery` → inner
product 30/30 and `nonvisuality` → inner product 30/30 — two different
directions landing on exactly the same technique, the one that abandons the
triangle altogether. `brevity` → similar triangles 29/30, `elementarity` →
rearrangement 29/30, `visuality` → rearrangement 27/30, `constructiveness`
→ rearrangement 25/30.

That `machinery` and `nonvisuality` coincide completely is the cleanest
demonstration in the project that these are not independent coordinates. On
this theorem, "use the heaviest machinery" and "avoid all pictures" are the
same instruction.

## An out-of-set check, and how it misled me

Nearest-centroid must return something. So calibrate: how close is a
technique-arm record to its own centroid, leave-one-out? Take the 5th
percentile as a threshold and ask how many extreme-arm records fall below.

On primes this reads as though six of eight directions leave the reference
set: `constructiveness` 30/30 below, `machinery` 29/30, `elementarity`
25/30, `visuality` 24/30, `nonvisuality` 23/30, `generality` 14/30.

**The other two theorems show this cannot mean what it appears to.** On √2
*everything* is out-of-set — 25/30 to 30/30 for every direction including
`brevity`, which on primes was 0/30. On Pythagoras only `machinery` (28/30)
and `surprise` (26/30) are, while `brevity` is 2/30 and `nonvisuality` 1/30.

The same measurement, on three corpora built the same way, gives three
incompatible pictures. Reading twelve primes proofs by hand had already told
me that only `machinery` genuinely leaves the reference set and the rest are
out-of-*register* — a formal, hedged, encyclopedic tone that moves a record
in embedding space without changing the mathematics. The cross-theorem
inconsistency is the quantitative version of that finding. **The out-of-set
threshold measures register, not scope.**

## The known failing check

Section 0 of the extreme-arm analysis tests whether language means estimated
on the *technique* arm remove language from the *extreme* arm. They do not,
and this is theorem-dependent:

| theorem | language probe, raw | centred | chance | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| primes | 0.996 | 0.429 | 0.167 | fails |
| √2 | 0.983 | 0.388 | 0.167 | fails |
| Pythagoras | 0.996 | 0.300 | 0.167 | passes |

So the extreme-arm results rest on the mapping being identical across all
six languages, not on clean out-of-sample language removal. That is stated
rather than patched, and it is why the per-language table above matters more
than the pooled χ².

## The scope test

Each scope target sits on a boundary where some known arguments stop
working. Classify them against the centroids without letting them touch any
estimate.

On primes the pattern is legible: `primes_2_mod_5` — where Murty's result
says *no* Euclidean proof exists, since 4 ≢ 1 mod 5 — draws Euler product
15/18, while `primes_3_mod_4`, where Euclid's argument adapts directly,
draws Euclid 17/18. The model's choice tracks the mathematics.

Pythagoras gives the cleanest signal in the project. `inner_product_space`
and `parallelogram_law` both land 18/18 on the inner-product centroid with
cosine 0.643 and 0.627 — the *only* scope targets anywhere that sit
comfortably inside the reference set (0/18 below threshold). Every other
target on every theorem is mostly out-of-set at cosine 0.03–0.34. When the
statement genuinely is the inner-product statement, the model produces the
inner-product proof and the instrument sees it.

# Part 3: a coordinate that is not wording

## The error, and the fix

Everything above measures a proof by where its *wording* lands. That
produced the central error: the embedding assigned 26/30 `machinery` records
to the Fürstenberg centroid, because Fürstenberg has the most
abstract-*vocabulary* proof in the reference set — while its mathematics is
Euclid's argument in topological dress, invoking no theorem at all.

A similarity measure cannot tell "uses abstract terminology" from "uses
powerful theorems". So count the second thing directly: the number of
distinct named external results a proof leans on.

The manipulation check works exactly as the prompts predict. On primes:

| direction | brevity | elementarity | nonvisuality | visuality | surprise | constructiveness | generality | machinery |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| invoked | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.03 | 0.13 | 0.13 | 1.27 | **16.63** |

And it makes the distinction the embedding could not:

- Fürstenberg (technique arm, n=60): mean **0.32**, median 0
- machinery (extreme arm, n=30): mean **16.63**, median 17.5
- best single threshold (≥2): separation accuracy **1.000**, against the
  embedding cosine's 0.133

**The length control is the one that matters.** The count correlates with
length (Spearman +0.586 on the extreme arm) — quoting theorems takes words.
So compare `machinery` against the *longest* 30 technique-arm records
instead of all of them: those sit at length z +1.45 against machinery's
+0.91, and invoke **1.40** against **16.63**. At equal or greater length the
reference records invoke a twelfth as much. It is not length wearing a hat.

It is also script-independent, which is not obvious for a name-matching
measure: mean absolute spread across the six languages is 0.62 results, and
the median CJK/Latin ratio is 0.84.

## The honest limitation

This coordinate has almost no resolution outside the one distinction it was
built to make. Seven of eight directions on primes sit at 0.00–0.13; only
`machinery` and `generality` are non-trivial. It decisively separates heavy
machinery from abstract vocabulary and does not order the other six
directions against each other at all.

> **Provisional on two theorems.** The √2 and Pythagoras registries were
> built from the literature rather than derived from a corpus, and the code
> prints a PROVISIONAL banner for them. Their numbers — machinery 8.27 vs
> analytic 0.47 on √2, 4.60 vs similar triangles 0.57 on Pythagoras — are
> reported here only as replication in *direction*, not as figures. Both
> also fall back to a stand-in contrast (the lowest-dependence technique)
> because the contrast that matters is whichever technique the *embedding*
> confuses with machinery, and that is corpus knowledge I have only entered
> for primes. The scale of the risk if a registry is wrong: the √2 registry
> run against the primes corpus reports 0.85 invoked results where the right
> one reports 16.63 — a recall failure that reads exactly like a null result.

## Two coordinates

Normalised length against invoked results, by direction, on primes:

| direction | length z | invoked |
|---|---|---|
| machinery | +0.91 | 16.63 |
| constructiveness | +0.44 | 0.13 |
| visuality | +0.34 | 0.03 |
| generality | +0.28 | 1.27 |
| surprise | +0.23 | 0.13 |
| elementarity | −0.10 | 0.00 |
| nonvisuality | −0.24 | 0.00 |
| brevity | −1.98 | 0.00 |

`brevity` and `elementarity` separate on coordinate 1 and coincide on
coordinate 2 — the same argument at different lengths, which is what reading
the proofs suggests. `constructiveness` and `generality` sit at nearly the
same length and are 10× apart in dependence.

That is two coordinates that mean something, which is the minimum for the
geometry in Conway and Shipman's framing to be worth taking literally. It is
also, on this evidence, the maximum.

## What I got wrong

Four things, all caught by a check that took under a minute to write:

1. **Near-orthogonal subspaces** as a claim about the design. The permuted
   null sits at the same angle (p = 0.86). It measures the dimension.
2. **"Technique structure survives translation"** as a claim about argument
   structure. A within-English TF-IDF baseline hits 0.940 against the
   encoder's 0.980 on primes — and on √2 it hits 1.000 against 0.933.
   Technique identity is largely lexical.
3. **"Most directions leave the reference set"**, inferred from an
   out-of-set cosine threshold. Reading twelve proofs showed only
   `machinery` does; the cross-theorem comparison above confirms the
   threshold is measuring register.
4. **The machinery/Fürstenberg conflation** — the embedding merged the arm
   with the most abstract vocabulary and the arm with the heaviest tools.
   Only a non-lexical coordinate separates them.

The nulls and baselines are the point. Each of them cost ten seconds and
each killed a headline.

## Limitations

- **The clouds interpenetrate.** Within-technique spread is 0.459–0.522
  while between-centroid distances are 0.354–0.500 — the spread is as large
  as the separation, for all but the top technique on each theorem. The
  extremal ranking ranks centroids; it fits no hull.
- **Probe accuracy is uninformative at full dimension.** 300 points with
  five near-duplicates per cell are trivially separable in 1024 dimensions.
  Made hard (PCA to 5 dimensions, one record per cell, n=60), technique
  falls to 0.717 and language to 0.133.
- **One generator, one encoder.** Everything here is a fact about
  `claude-opus-5` and `bge-m3`. The additivity result in particular could
  still be a property of this encoder.
- **Proof correctness is unverified** beyond twelve records read by hand.
  Nothing in the pipeline checks it. On Pythagoras this is a live risk: the
  famous wrong proof derives the theorem from sin² + cos² = 1, which *is*
  the theorem, and the `surprise` and `machinery` directions need
  hand-reading.
- **Coverage.** Loomis catalogued 371 proofs of Pythagoras, so six centroids
  cover far less of the known space there than five did for primes, and the
  out-of-set threshold is correspondingly weaker evidence.
- **Two direction prompts are not comparable across theorems.**
  `elementarity` says "assume nothing beyond the definition of divisibility",
  which is right for primes and √2 and irrelevant to areas;
  `constructiveness` asks for "a strictly simpler representation", which is
  the √2 descent. Both were adapted. So brevity, generality, visuality,
  machinery and surprise stay comparable across all three; elementarity
  across primes and √2 only; constructiveness not across theorems at all.

## What would come next

**Embed the masked corpora.** It is the one measurement that would settle
whether the encoder reads arguments or vocabulary, the code writes the
corpora already, and it is six embedding runs away. Everything in Part 1
after "language is removable" is provisional until that number exists.

**Derive the √2 and Pythagoras registries from their corpora** rather than
from the literature, and record the real contrast technique for each. Part 3
replicates in direction on both, but the figures should not be quoted until
the registries have been checked the way the primes one was.

**A second generator.** One model gives one space. The interesting claim —
that brevity and machinery oppose each other the same way regardless of what
is being proved — now has three theorems behind it and one model. The model
is the remaining single point of failure, and everything after generation
runs on cached files in about two minutes, so the cost is one more batch job.
